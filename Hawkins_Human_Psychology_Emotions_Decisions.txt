HOW THIS FILE IS BUILT
----------------------
Parts 1 through 12 contain only ideas, claims, examples, and quotations taken
from the book. Chapter references are given in [brackets] so you can check
them. Quotations are copied from the text. Everything else is a close
paraphrase. No outside psychology, marketing theory, or other authors have
been added except where Hawkins himself cites them (Freud, Jung, Frankl,
Robert Ringer, Alice Bailey, etc.).

Part 13 is the only section that is NOT from the book. It is clearly labeled.
It translates the book's principles into advertising terms, so you can see
which conclusions are Hawkins' and which are an application of them.

Note: Hawkins presents several claims (muscle-testing, energy fields, psychic
connection, "what you hold in mind tends to manifest") as established facts.
They are reported here as his views and are not independently verified.

PART 1 - THE CORE MODEL: WHAT ACTUALLY DRIVES A HUMAN BEING
===============================================================================

1.1 Survival is the master goal  [Ch. 3]
-----------------------------------------
Hawkins says that whichever psychology you study, it shows that "the primary
human goal, superseding all others, is survival." Every human desire tries to
secure:
  - one's personal survival, and
  - the survival of one's identified groups (family, loved ones, country).

What humans fear most is "the loss of the capacity to experience." People
protect the body because they believe they ARE the body.

1.2 The sense of lack  [Ch. 3]
------------------------------
Because people see themselves as separate and limited, "they are stressed by
their sense of lack." It is common for humans to "look outside of themselves
for the satisfaction of their needs." This makes them feel vulnerable,
"because they are insufficient unto themselves."

KEY IDEA: The default human psychology is a felt incompleteness that people
try to fill with things, people, status, and approval from outside.

1.3 The mind is a survival machine that runs on emotion  [Ch. 3]
----------------------------------------------------------------
"The mind is, therefore, a survival mechanism, and its method of survival is
primarily the use of emotions."

  - Emotions produce thoughts.
  - Over time, emotions become "shorthand for thoughts."
  - "Thousands and even millions of thoughts can be replaced by a single
    emotion."
  - "Emotions are more basic and primitive than mental processes."

1.4 There are only two real goals  [Ch. 3]
------------------------------------------
"Every activity or desire will reveal that the basic goal is to achieve a
certain feeling. There are no other goals than to overcome fear and achieve
happiness."

And: "Emotions are connected with what we believe will ensure our survival,
not with what actually will."

1.5 All negative feelings are forms of fear  [Ch. 2, Ch. 18]
------------------------------------------------------------
  - "All negative feelings are associated with our basic fear related to
    survival," and "all feelings are merely survival programs that the mind
    believes are necessary."  [Ch. 2]
  - "All of the negative feelings are essentially forms of fear: fear of loss
    of esteem by ourselves or others, or fear of not surviving and a loss of
    security."  [Ch. 18]

So under every negative emotion there are two basic fears:
  (a) fear of losing ESTEEM (self-worth, approval, respect), and
  (b) fear of losing SECURITY or SURVIVAL.

1.6 The small self vs. the greater Self  [Ch. 3, 4, 7]
------------------------------------------------------
Hawkins divides every person into two parts.

THE SMALL SELF (ego): "the part of us that is mean, petty, selfish,
competitive, cheap, conniving, mistrusting, vindictive, judgmental,
diminished, weak, guilty, ashamed, and vain." It:
  - clings to negative emotions,
  - is "very willing to accept negative programming,"
  - "glorifies in how miserable life is,"
  - loves making itself and others "wrong,"
  - is attached to the familiar, no matter how painful (Foreword).

THE GREATER SELF (Higher Self): the "composite of our higher feelings."
It "loves rather than seeks love." It has generosity, forgiveness, courage,
and "inner greatness."

"The world can only see us as we see ourselves."  [Ch. 3]

1.7 The payoffs of negativity  [Ch. 3, 4, 7, 8]
-----------------------------------------------
Hawkins repeats that people hold on to negative feelings because those
feelings pay them something:

  "Anger. Hatred. Self-pity. Resentments. They all have their cheap little
  payoff, that little inner satisfaction... There is a weird, quirky
  pleasure when we hang on to pain."  [Ch. 3]

The payoffs of BLAME [Ch. 4]: "We get to be innocent; we get to enjoy
self-pity; we get to be the martyr and the victim; and we get to be the
recipients of sympathy." The biggest is: "we get to be the innocent victim
and the other party is the bad one."

"Blame is the world's greatest excuse. It enables us to remain limited and
small without feeling guilty."  [Ch. 4]

1.8 Why people hang on to a feeling  [Ch. 18, Ch. 21]
-----------------------------------------------------
"If we get stuck in a feeling, it is because we still secretly believe that
it will accomplish something for us."  [Ch. 18]

The most frequent cause of resistance to letting go: "We think that somehow,
if we hang on to that feeling, it is going to get us what we want." Usually
it is "a fantasy that it will have some effect on some other person and
change their behavior or attitudes toward us."  [Ch. 21]

PART 2 - FEELINGS RUN THOUGHTS; REASON SERVES EMOTION
===============================================================================

2.1 Feelings cause thoughts, not the other way around  [Preface, Ch. 2]
-----------------------------------------------------------------------
  - "The mind, with its thoughts, is driven by feelings. Each feeling is the
    cumulative derivative of many thousands of thoughts."  [Preface]
  - "It is the accumulated pressure of feelings that causes thoughts. One
    feeling, for instance, can create literally thousands of thoughts over a
    period of time."  [Ch. 2]
  - "It is not thoughts or facts that are painful but the feelings that
    accompany them."  [Ch. 2]

2.2 Memory is filed by feeling, not by fact  [Ch. 2, Ch. 3]
-----------------------------------------------------------
Citing the Gray-LaViolette research, Hawkins says thoughts are filed in the
memory bank "according to the various shades of feelings associated with
those thoughts." They are "filed according to feeling tone, not fact."
So self-awareness grows much faster by watching feelings than by watching
thoughts.

2.3 Reason is the servant of emotion  [Ch. 3]
---------------------------------------------
"Reason is the tool the mind uses to achieve its emotional ends."

When the underlying emotion is out of awareness, "people are unaware of the
reason for their actions and they develop all kinds of plausible reasons. In
fact, they frequently do not know why they are doing what they are doing."

Thoughts are "merely rationalizations of the mind to try and explain the
presence of the feeling."  [Ch. 2]

2.4 The "What for?" technique: finding the real motive  [Ch. 3]
---------------------------------------------------------------
To find the emotional goal behind any activity, ask "What for?" over and
over until you reach the basic feeling. Hawkins' own example:

  A man wants a new Cadillac. His mind gives logical reasons, "but logic
  doesn't really explain it."
    - What do I want the Cadillac for?  -> status, recognition, respect,
                                          "solid citizen success status"
    - What do I want status for?        -> respect and approval from others
    - What do I want respect for?       -> to have the feeling of security
    - What do I want security for?      -> to feel happy

  Result: underneath are "feelings of insecurity, unhappiness, and lack of
  fulfillment."

The same method applied to MONEY [Ch. 17]: Write "Money" at the top of a
page and list everything it means: "security, power, glamour, sexual
attraction, successful competition, self-worth, and our value to others and
the world." Then "money in and of itself is not the most basic issue. More
important than money itself are the emotional gratifications that we hope
will be ours with the use of that money."

Example: behind the desire for money we discover "one of our goals is to be
respected and valued... it isn't money that we want at all but the
self-respect and esteem we thought it would bring us."

2.5 The unconscious "genie" takes orders  [Ch. 7]
-------------------------------------------------
"Our unconscious genie, which can only take orders and not make decisions,"
carries out whatever belief we hold. The woman who kept saying "My
relationships never work out" was, in Hawkins' view, giving the order that
produced the outcome.

"The unconscious will allow us to have only what we think we deserve."
[Ch. 7]  "The unconscious brings to us what it thinks we deserve."  [Ch. 17]

2.6 The unconscious is crude  [Ch. 6]
-------------------------------------
"The unconscious mind is not polite. It thinks in gross concepts." When
someone cuts you off in traffic, the honest uncensored image is to "run them
off the road." "While your intellect went to prep school, your unconscious
remained in the jungle where it is still swinging in the trees!" These are
symbols, not intentions, and are "comical once we look at them."

PART 3 - THE THREE WAYS PEOPLE HANDLE FEELINGS
===============================================================================
[Ch. 2 - "Feelings and Mental Mechanisms"]

People handle feelings in three main ways: SUPPRESSION, EXPRESSION, ESCAPE.

3.1 Suppression and repression
------------------------------
  - Repression is unconscious; suppression is conscious.
  - Which feelings get pushed down follows "the conscious and unconscious
    programs that we carry within us from social custom and family
    training."
  - Suppressed pressure comes out as irritability, mood swings, muscle
    tension, headaches, insomnia, hypertension, and other symptoms.
  - Repression is kept in place mainly by DENIAL and PROJECTION (see Part 4).

3.2 Expression
--------------
  - Venting lets out "just enough of the inner pressure... so that the
    remainder can then be suppressed."
  - "The expression of a feeling, first, tends to propagate that feeling and
    give it greater energy."
  - Dumping feelings on others is experienced by them as an attack, so it
    "results in the deterioration and destruction of relationships."
  - Hawkins says expression became fashionable through a misreading of Freud.
    Freud actually said repressed feelings should be "neutralized,
    sublimated, socialized, and channeled into constructive drives of love,
    work and creativity."

3.3 Escape (diversion)
----------------------
"Escape is the avoidance of feelings through diversion. This avoidance is
the backbone of the entertainment and liquor industries, and also the route
of the workaholic. Escapism and avoidance of inner awareness is a socially
condoned mechanism."

"People are desperate to stay unconscious... People are terrified of facing
themselves. They dread even a moment of aloneness. Thus the constant frantic
activities: the endless socializing, talking, texting, reading, music
playing, working, traveling, sightseeing, shopping, overeating, gambling,
movie-going, pill-taking, drug-using, and cocktail-partying."

Many of these pursuits "eventually become addictions as our dependency upon
them grows."

3.4 The daily escape routine  [Ch. 17 - "Happiness"]
----------------------------------------------------
Hawkins describes the average day as one long escape. Radio or TV goes on
the moment we wake up, then grooming and the body, then the schedule,
calls, texts, and news, then work with "the business, the deals, the
bargains... the endless search for power, the quest for 'strokes,' and the
ever-present fear of survival." When this stops, we face "the internal
emptiness," which "calls for the incessant ingestion of novels, magazines,
television, and websites... parties... drugs... a few drinks... movies."

"All of this is motivated by the desire to somehow derive meaning and
security, and to increase our self-esteem and ensure our self-worth by
whatever means."

He adds: "There is nothing wrong with any of these activities, in and of
themselves." What matters is the state of consciousness behind them.

3.5 The fourth option: letting go  [Ch. 2]
------------------------------------------
Letting go means being aware of a feeling, "letting it come up, staying with
it, and letting it run its course without wanting to make it different or do
anything about it." "It is resistance that keeps the feeling going." A
feeling that is not resisted "will disappear as the energy behind it
dissipates."

To be surrendered means: "It's okay if it happens, and it's okay if it
doesn't." "We can enjoy a thing, but we don't need it for our happiness."

PART 4 - PROJECTION, STRESS, AND "WE SEE WHAT WE HOLD INSIDE"
===============================================================================

4.1 Projection  [Ch. 2]
-----------------------
Out of guilt and fear we deny a feeling in ourselves and project it outward.
"'They' then become the enemy, and the mind searches for and finds
justification to reinforce the projection." Blame lands on people, places,
institutions, food, weather, fate, God, luck, foreigners, political rivals.

"Projection is the main mechanism in use by the world today." Hawkins says it
underlies "all attack, violence, aggression, and every form of social
destruction." "We maintain our own self-esteem at the expense of others."

4.2 Stress is internal  [Ch. 2, Ch. 14]
---------------------------------------
"The real source of 'stress' is actually internal; it is not external, as
people would like to believe." "The readiness to react with fear... depends
on how much fear is already present within to be triggered by a stimulus."

"It is not the external stimulus, then, that is the cause of stress, but our
degree of reactivity."

External events are "merely the straws that eventually break the camel's
back. The main stress load is what we carry around with us all the time."
[Ch. 14]

4.3 We perceive the world through our dominant feeling  [Ch. 2]
---------------------------------------------------------------
  "To the fearful person, this world is a terrifying place. To the angry
  person, this world is a chaos of frustration and vexation. To the guilty
  person, it is a world of temptation and sin... What we are holding inside
  colors our world... The basic rule is that we focus on what we have
  repressed."

4.4 Displacement: events are triggers, not causes  [Ch. 2]
----------------------------------------------------------
People say "They made me angry." Hawkins: "Actually, it's the exact
opposite. The suppressed and repressed feelings seek an outlet and utilize
the events as triggers and excuses to vent themselves. We are like
pressure-cookers... It is because we are angry that events 'make' us angry."

When pressure exceeds tolerance, the mind creates an outside event to vent
on: "the person with a lot of repressed grief will unconsciously create sad
events... The fearful person precipitates frightening experiences; the angry
person becomes surrounded by infuriating circumstances; and the prideful
person is constantly being insulted."

4.5 Like attracts like  [Ch. 2, Ch. 3, Ch. 6]
---------------------------------------------
"The basic rule of the psychic universe is that 'like attracts like.'"
"Anger attracts angry thoughts... love promotes love." "Fear attracts fear
just as its corollary is true that love attracts love."

Hawkins links this to prosperity: "people who are carrying the consciousness
of apathy bring poverty circumstances into their lives, and those with a
prosperity consciousness bring abundance into their lives."

4.6 People sense your inner state  [Ch. 2, Ch. 18]
--------------------------------------------------
Hawkins asserts we "routinely and unwittingly affect others by our emotional
state and thoughts." Animals "instantly read a person's basic emotional
state."

In relationships: "a very quick way of learning is to presume that the
other person is conscious and aware of our inner thoughts and feelings...
They will respond to us as if they knew our inner feelings."

"Others always know when we are out to exploit them."  [Ch. 18]

PART 5 - THE SCALE OF EMOTIONS (MAP OF CONSCIOUSNESS), LEVEL BY LEVEL
===============================================================================
[Ch. 3 - "The Scale of Emotions," plus chapters 4-13 for each level]

5.1 The basic structure
-----------------------
  - Every level is calibrated on a logarithmic scale from 1 to 1000.
  - Shame (20) is at the bottom, "close to death, representing bare
    survival."
  - Full Enlightenment (1000) is at the top.
  - COURAGE (200) is the critical dividing line: "the shift from negative to
    positive energy." Below 200 is destructive ("force"), and above 200 is
    life-supportive ("power").

  "Above the level of Courage, people seek us out because we give energy to
  them ('power') and we have goodwill towards them. Below the level of
  Courage, people avoid us because we take energy from them ('force') and we
  want to use them for our own material or emotional needs."

  "The fastest way to move from the bottom to the top is by telling the
  truth to ourselves and to others."

5.2 The levels, from lowest to highest
--------------------------------------
For each level: the book's description, the inner voice or phrases, what it
wants from others (Ch. 18), and other notes from the level's own chapter.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
SHAME (20)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "Characterized by humiliation, as in 'hanging your head in
  shame.' It is traditionally accompanied by banishment. It is destructive
  to health and leads to cruelty toward self and others."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
GUILT (30)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "one wants to punish and be punished. It leads to
  self-rejection, masochism, remorse, 'feeling bad,' and self-sabotage.
  'It's all my fault.'" It is the basis of many psychosomatic illnesses.

  Ch. 6 details:
  - Guilt is "a particular form of fear" always tied to "a feeling of
    wrongness and potential punishment."
  - "Ninety-nine percent of guilt has nothing whatsoever to do with
    reality."
  - "Guilt is as prevalent as fear, and we feel guilty no matter what we
    are doing." There is always a "should": better golf score, read a book
    instead of TV, cook better, be smarter, be stronger.
  - "In between the fear of living and the fear of dying is the guilt of
    the moment."
  - Guilt underlies "our willingness to believe negative things about
    ourselves."
  - "The happiness and joy of the day is instantly destroyed by one negative
    remark."

  What guilt seeks from others (Ch. 18): "to placate, to assuage, to escape
  punishment by self-punishment, and to elicit forgiveness" and, most of
  all, to elicit punishment. Guilt tends to draw criticism from others.

  On success (Ch. 7): "out of guilt we strive to accomplish and achieve
  success. Then, when we achieve success, we feel guilty because we have it.
  There is no winning of the guilt game."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
APATHY (50)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "hopelessness, playing dead, being a 'drain' to others,
  being immobilized, and the feelings: 'I can't' and 'Who cares?' Poverty is
  common."

  Phrases (Ch. 4): "Who cares?" "What's the use?" "It's boring." "Why
  bother?" "I can't win anyway." "Won't do any good anyway." "Too hard."
  "Too late." "Too old." "Too young."

  Biological purpose: "to summon aid, but part of the feeling is that no
  help is possible."

  Hawkins says much of the world's population functions at this level.

  KEY INSIGHT (Ch. 4): "most 'I can'ts' are really 'I won'ts.'" Behind them
  is usually fear ("I am afraid to"), shame ("I am ashamed to"), or pride
  ("I have too much pride to try, for fear I might fail").

  Apathy is "the price we pay for having settled for and bought into our
  smallness."

  What it seeks from others (Ch. 18): "to elicit sympathy, to win back, to
  gain support, to make others sorry, and to summon help." Others respond
  with help, then pity, "and finally avoidance," because of "the huge
  energy demand."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
GRIEF (75)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "helplessness, despair, loss, regret, and the feeling, 'If
  only I had...' Separation. Depression. Sadness. Being a 'loser.'
  Mournful, as in 'I can't go on.'"

  Phrases (Ch. 5): "All the years I've wasted." "I'll never get over
  this." "I tried, but nothing helps."

  Difference from apathy: grief is "a crying for someone to help because we
  can't do anything about it, and we feel that maybe someone else can do it
  for us." In apathy, there is "a feeling that no one can help."

  Grief can be triggered by "the loss of a belief system, a relationship, a
  capacity or role, a hope about ourselves, or an overall attitude."

  ROOT CAUSE: "The psychological basis of all grief and mourning is
  attachment." Things we use to fill an inner need become "mine," then an
  "extension of 'me.'" Losing them feels like losing part of ourselves.
  "Attachment creates a dependency, and dependency... intrinsically carries
  with it a fear of loss."

  Grief is time-limited: if fully surrendered to, it "will run out in about
  10-20 minutes" at a time.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FEAR (100)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "This energy sees 'danger,' which is 'everywhere.' It is
  avoidant, defensive, preoccupied with security, possessive of others,
  jealous, restless, anxious, and vigilant."

  Ch. 6 list of fears: fear of pain, living, loving, closeness, rejection,
  failure, poverty, ridicule and criticism, being trapped, inadequacy,
  disapproval, boredom, responsibility, making a decision, authority,
  punishment, change, loss of security, losing control, being found out,
  and "fear of fear itself."

  - "Fear is so pandemic in our society that it constitutes the predominant
    ruling emotion of our world."
  - "For most people, fear is so all-pervasive that their life really
    constitutes one giant set of compensatory devices to conquer their
    fears."
  - Fear was the predominant emotion among his thousands of patients.
  - "Fears tend to escalate," spreading into more areas of life.
  - The media repeat fearful situations "as though to give the mind further
    opportunities to master that most dreaded of all emotions."
  - We hold on to fear because of "the unconscious fantasy that fear is
    keeping us alive." Hawkins: "Fear is what blinds us to the real dangers
    of life."
  - A hidden fear: "the fear of retaliation," which comes from our own
    desire to strike back.

  What fear seeks (Ch. 18): "escape from the imagined threat, and to put
  psychological distance from the feared situation or person." In
  relationships, the insecure person becomes jealous, clinging, and
  possessive, which pressures the other person, who "has an inner impulse to
  run for freedom."

  "Fear in relationships, therefore, is giving away our power to another
  person."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
DESIRE (125)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "always seeking gain, acquisition, pleasure, and 'getting'
  something outside oneself. It is insatiable, never satisfied, and craving.
  'I have to have it.' 'Give me what I want, and give it to me now!'"

  Ch. 7 list: greed, obsession, hunger, envy, jealousy, clinging, hoarding,
  fixation, over-ambition, lust, possessiveness, control, glamorization,
  insatiability, acquisitiveness. "Never satisfied." "Never enough."
  "Must have."

  "The underlying quality of this emotion is its drivenness. When we are at
  the effect of desire, we are no longer free."

  See PART 7 for the full treatment.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ANGER (150)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "overcomes the source of fear by force, threats, and attack.
  It is irritable, explosive, bitter, volatile, and resentful. It likes to
  'get even,' as in 'I'll show you.'"

  Ch. 8 list: rage, resentment, revenge, outrage, indignation, jealousy,
  spite, hatred, contempt, sarcasm, impatience, frustration, rebellion,
  sullenness, pouting, stubbornness.

  - "There is a lot of energy in anger; therefore, we may actually feel
    energized when we are irritated or angry."
  - "In anger, there is the energy for action. This results in doingness in
    the world."
  - "When the 'have-nots' of the world become energized by desire and move
    up to anger over what they lack, that anger moves them into the actions
    necessary to fulfill their dreams for a better life."
  - "It's better to be angry than apathetic by a long shot."  [Ch. 4]
  - Media violence shows how much suppressed anger is in the population.
  - We feel so guilty about anger that we need to make its object "wrong"
    so our anger is "justified."
  - Sources of anger: fear, and pride, especially vanity and the "pride of
    self-sacrifice."
  - "Anger kills the angry person, not the so-called 'enemy.'"
  - "Anger is binding, not freeing."
  - Q&A (Ch. 21): "If you look at anger, you will see that its basis is
    almost always fear... Anger is coming from weakness rather than
    strength."

  Using anger positively (Ch. 8): instead of venting at the boss, use the
  energy to "create a project that, because of its excellence, proves our
  point," to find a better job, or to take a course.

  What anger seeks (Ch. 18): "to punish others, make them feel sorry, try
  to force them to change their feelings or behavior, make them suffer, get
  even." Others respond with "counter-criticism, counter-resentment, and
  avoidance."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PRIDE (175)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "'My way is the best way'... Its focus is achievement,
  desire for recognition, specialness, and perfectionism. It feels 'better
  than...' and superior to others."

  See PART 8 for the full treatment.

  What pride seeks (Ch. 18): "to win their admiration, avoid criticism or
  rejection, gain acceptance, be important, and thereby overcome our own
  inner feelings of worthlessness." Others respond with "envy,
  competitiveness, or even hatred and easy exploitation."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
=================== COURAGE (200): THE TURNING POINT ===================
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "This energy says, 'I can do it.' It is determined, excited
  about life, productive, independent, and self-empowered. Effective action
  is possible."

  Ch. 10 details:
  - "The hallmark of courage is the knowledge and feeling, 'I can.'"
  - Feels assured, skillful, adequate, capable, alive, loving and giving,
    with "an overall zest for life."
  - "People on the level of courage are the doers of the world."
  - "The levels of consciousness up to this point are concerned primarily
    with gain." At courage, other people "are no longer being looked at
    primarily as a means of help, survival, or support."
  - "Because of the inner self-confidence, we are much less concerned with
    security."
  - "There is the willingness to take chances and to let go of former
    securities."
  - Can admit mistakes "without indulging in guilt."
  - Willing to take self-improvement courses and "risk shifting
    paradigms."
  - Phrases: "I can handle it." "We'll make it." "The job will get done."
    "We can see this through." "All things shall pass."
  - People here want "jobs which will be of some benefit to the world" and
    "more meaning to their job than just a salary."
  - "Money is not spent solely for self-gratification, self-aggrandizement,
    or self-fortification; rather, it is seen as a tool for
    accomplishment."
  - "Success breeds success" comes from this level.
  - "It is often easy to jump from any of the lower feelings up to courage
    merely by affirming our courage to look at and handle our feelings."
  - Self-esteem rises from facing a fear "whether the fear disappears or
    not."

  What courage elicits in others (Ch. 18): "cooperation, courage, respect,
  and a willingness to be with us... because we increase their self-esteem,
  they seek our company."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
NEUTRALITY (250)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "comfortable, pragmatic, and relatively free of
  emotionality. 'It's okay either way.' It is free of rigid positions,
  nonjudgmental, and noncompetitive."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WILLINGNESS (310)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "subserves survival by virtue of a positive attitude that
  welcomes all expressions of life. It is friendly, helpful, wants to
  assist, and seeks to be of service."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
ACCEPTANCE (350)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "easy-going, laid back, harmonious, flexible, inclusive, and
  free of inner resistance. 'Life is good. You and I are good. I feel
  connected.' It meets life on life's terms. There is no need to blame
  others or blame life."

  Ch. 11 details:
  - "I'm okay," "You're okay," "It's okay."
  - "a feeling of belonging, connectedness, fullness, loving, understanding,
    and the feeling of being understood."
  - "generosity and ease of giving, with no expectation of return or record
    keeping."
  - "everyone is actually doing the best they can with what they have at
    the moment."
  - Love is seen as coming from within. In desire, by contrast, "we are
    looking to be loved. It seems to be something we 'get.'"
  - No moralistic "good" and "bad"; "It just becomes obvious what works and
    what doesn't work."
  - "There is certainty that our own personal needs will be met."
  - "What becomes increasingly important is what we are becoming, not what
    we have or do."
  - Acceptance differs from RESIGNATION. Resignation says, "I don't like it,
    but I have to put up with it." "One of the signs of acceptance is
    serenity."  [Ch. 5]

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
REASON (400)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "the ability to see things in the abstract, to conceptualize,
  to be objective, and to make rapid and correct decisions. Its enormous
  utility is problem solving. Science, philosophy, medicine, and logic are
  expressions of this level."

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
LOVE (500)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "forgiving, nurturing, and supportive. It does not proceed
  from the mind; rather, it emanates from the heart. Love focuses on the
  essence of a situation, not the details."

  Ch. 12 details:
  - "Love is a way of being... It is a way of being in the world that says:
    'How can I be of help to you?'"
  - "That which we freely give to life flows back to us."
  - "What we affirm in others, we actually affirm in ourselves."
  - "Once you become loving... people do things for you that they would
    not do for others."
  - "Sometimes, it is best not to tell people that you love them because
    they'll get scared and think that you have designs on them or you want
    something from them."
  - "Love augments the positive about others rather than their defects."
  - "Love makes no demands."
  - Ch. 16: "For love, people will go to any lengths and do things that they
    would never do for any amount of money."
  - Ch. 18: "what would we be willing to do for someone we really, truly
    love? ...almost anything. There is almost no limit to which we will go
    for love. Now contrast this with what we are willing to do for someone
    who has intimidated us... we grudgingly give as little as possible."

  Romantic love is "only a minor part of a human lifetime." Hawkins lists
  many forms: love of pets, family, freedom, purpose, country, creation;
  love as enthusiasm, forgiveness, appreciation, kindness, loyalty,
  friendship, respect, devotion, fraternal bonds (buddies, teammates).

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
JOY (540) - Unconditional Love
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "Love that is unconditional and unchanging, despite
  circumstances and actions of others... A sense of self-completion and
  self-sufficiency prevails." Hawkins says this occurs in only .04% of the
  population.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PEACE (600)
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Description: "perfection, bliss, effortlessness, and oneness." "The person
  who has found inner peace can no longer be intimidated, controlled,
  manipulated, or programmed."  [Ch. 13]

5.3 General rules of the scale  [Ch. 3]
---------------------------------------
  - Lower levels go with "lower energy, lower power, poorer life
    circumstances, poorer relationships, less abundance, less love, and
    poorer physical and emotional health."
  - "Such needy people drain us on all levels. They tend to be avoided and
    find themselves surrounded by people on the same level."
  - Higher people "give off life energy to all living things around them...
    We say they are 'high.'"
  - "That which is impossible to see or experience at lower levels of
    consciousness becomes self-evident and stunningly obvious at higher
    levels."

5.4 How each level sees God (a window into each worldview)  [Ch. 10]
--------------------------------------------------------------------
  Apathy -> hopeless.  Grief -> hopelessly separated from help.
  Guilt  -> undeserving; expects punishment rather than love.
  Fear   -> fearful, punishing, avenging, jealous, angry.
  Anger  -> depriving, arbitrary, capricious, failing.
  Pride  -> rigid, exclusive, bigoted, argumentative.
  Courage-> takes responsibility; seeks meaning by choice.

Hawkins says these levels color how a person sees everything, not just God.
At lower levels "the universe is seen as negative and denying, frustrating,
and reluctant. It is like a bad, stingy parent." At higher levels "it now
becomes like a giving, loving, unconditionally approving parent."  [Ch. 7]


===============================================================================
PART 6 - HOW PEOPLE MOVE UP (AND DOWN) THE SCALE
===============================================================================

6.1 The public-speaking ladder  [Ch. 4]
---------------------------------------
Hawkins traces one person climbing every level in one area of life:

  APATHY : "Oh, I can't possibly speak in public... Nobody will want to hear
           me anyway."
  FEAR   : Remembering the intention shows the apathy covers fear. Now it
           is "frightening, not hopeless." "The facts are not that we
           'can't,' but merely that we are 'afraid.'"
  DESIRE : As fear is let go, "we have a desire to do the very thing that
           we fear." (Grief over lost past opportunities may come up too.)
  ANGER  : Anger at the fear that blocked us. "In anger there is much more
           energy and capacity for action." This leads to a DECISION to act.
  PRIDE  : Signing up for a course: "we have finally taken the bull by the
           horns."
  COURAGE: Facing renewed fear on the way: "we have courage in our capacity
           at least to face our fears."
  ACCEPTANCE: "I can do it." Pleasure. Awareness of others' embarrassment
           and concern for them.
  LOVE/PEACE: Compassion, loss of self-consciousness, wanting to share the
           journey with others.

"Fear is a higher energy state than apathy. Fear at least begins to motivate
us into action."

"One of the tricks people learn is to move up quickly from apathy and grief
into anger, and then to jump from anger to pride, and then on into
courage."  [Ch. 8]

6.2 Breakthroughs spread  [Ch. 4, Ch. 6, Ch. 16]
------------------------------------------------
  - Success in one area "spills over into other areas of life, and the
    increase in confidence results in greater material abundance."  [Ch. 4]
  - "As one fear is relinquished, all fear is diminished
    non-specifically."  [Ch. 16]
  - The man who could not dance went "from apathy to love" in an instant
    on the dance floor, and the breakthrough "transferred itself to many
    other previously blocked areas of his life."  [Ch. 16]

6.3 Crisis as an opportunity to go up or down  [Ch. 3]
------------------------------------------------------
"Every emotional experience is an opportunity to go up or down. Which do we
choose?" A crisis forces us to "stop all of our diversionary games" and
re-evaluate "our beliefs, goals, values, and life direction."

Big emotions are composites. The man who lost his job felt overwhelming
despair. Taken apart, it held anger, pride, resentment, self-invalidation,
and fear. Each piece can be handled on its own. Once the fear of not finding
another job is let go, "all the alternate possibilities that exist will
become suddenly apparent to him."

6.4 Choosing the meaning: re-contextualizing  [Ch. 3, Ch. 4]
------------------------------------------------------------
"One of the most effective tools for handling the past is the creation of a
different context. What this means is that we give it a different meaning."
Hawkins cites Viktor Frankl: "the last of the human freedoms - to choose
one's attitude in any given set of circumstances."

"Every life experience, no matter how 'tragic,' contains a hidden lesson."

6.5 The power of choice  [Ch. 4]
--------------------------------
"It is a totally different situation to see that we choose to blame rather
than to think that we have to blame."

"One of the laws of consciousness is: We are only subject to a negative
thought or belief if we consciously say that it applies to us."

Example: news says "No jobs are available." We can respond, "Unemployment
does not apply to me." During one flu epidemic, 8 of 14 people got sick, "but
six did not!" During the Depression, some people still became millionaires.

6.6 The company we keep  [Ch. 4]
--------------------------------
"we are either positively or negatively influenced by the company we keep.
It is unlikely that we will overcome an inhibition if we choose to be in
the company of others who have our same problem."

Self-help groups call this "hanging out with the winners" and "getting it by
osmosis." Hawkins tells of a bitter divorced woman who left a group of
bitter divorced friends and spent time with people who had rebuilt their
love lives. Her ulcer and migraines cleared up and she fell in love again.

6.7 Having -> Doing -> Being  [Ch. 7]
-------------------------------------
"The general progression of the levels of consciousness, as we go from the
lowest to the highest, is to move from havingness to doingness to
beingness."

  HAVING (lower levels): "it is what we have that counts... what we have
    that gives us our self-image of worth and position in the world."
  DOING (middle): once basic needs are met, "what we do in the world is the
    basis of our value and how others rate us." Over time doing becomes
    service.
  BEING (higher): "it is no longer what we do in the world but what we are
    that counts... People now seek our company... because of what we have
    become." We are "described as a charismatic person."

6.8 The mind discounts its own progress  [Ch. 2, Ch. 7, Ch. 17]
---------------------------------------------------------------
"the mind has a peculiar tendency to discount the very technique that
brought about the change." A man promoted from a 23-year-stuck position to
President credited "shifts in business patterns." Another credited his
brother-in-law for a job, not his own surrender: "It's a good thing I didn't
wait for God!" Hawkins: "the propensity of the mind is to disown one's own
power and project it elsewhere onto the world."

The mind also ridicules higher states it has not reached: "The mind will get
critical and try to save face by ridiculing a higher state."  [Ch. 17]


===============================================================================
PART 7 - DESIRE, GLAMOUR, STATUS, AND WANTING
===============================================================================
[Ch. 7 - "Desire"; Ch. 17 - "Wealth" and "Happiness"]

7.1 Desire blocks getting
-------------------------
"The main illusion is seen in the statement, 'The only way that I'll get what
I want is by desiring it; if I let go of my desire, then I won't get what I
want.' Actually, the opposite is true. Desire, especially strong desire
(e.g., cravingness), frequently blocks our getting what we want."

Why: "desire literally means, 'I do not have.'" It "put[s] a psychic distance
between ourselves and what we want." Wanting "results in a fear of not
getting it."

What actually brings things: "the way something comes into our life is
because we have chosen it. It was the result of our intention, or we made a
decision for it."

7.2 The Protestant-ethic belief  [Foreword, Ch. 2, Ch. 7]
---------------------------------------------------------
Common beliefs Hawkins says block people:
  (1) "We only deserve things through hard work, struggle, sacrifice, and
      effort"
  (2) "Suffering is beneficial and good for us"
  (3) "We don't get anything for nothing"
  (4) "Things that are very simple aren't worth much."  [Ch. 2]

If someone becomes successful almost effortlessly, "people are envious. It
really annoys them that we didn't have to go through all kinds of anguish."
[Ch. 7]

The foreword names the opposite belief that blocks happiness: "'There's got
to be a catch'; 'It's too good to be true'; 'It can happen for others but not
for me.'"

7.3 GLAMOUR: the gap between the thing and the fantasy  [Ch. 7]
---------------------------------------------------------------
This is the book's most direct discussion of advertising.

"If we look at something that we want, we can begin to distinguish between
the thing itself versus the aura, patina, flash, and attractive magnetic
effect of a quality that can best be described as 'glamour.' It is this
disparity between what a thing is in itself, and the glamour that we have
attached to it, which leads to disillusionment."

"Glamour means that we have attached sentimentality or we have made it
bigger than life. We have projected onto a thing a magical quality that
somehow leads us to believe that, once we acquire it, we will magically
achieve some higher state of happiness and satisfaction."

Examples:
  - The executive who climbs to company president expecting admiration and
    finds "viciousness, competitiveness, envy," exhaustion, and damaged
    relationships.
  - The woman who buys an expensive designer dress expecting adulation and
    gets "a few passing comments" and envious looks, then has the same
    argument with her escort on the way home.
  - Emotional events (a reunion, a first date, an election) are glamorized.
    "After the event passes, life goes on the same and disappointment
    ensues."

ON ADVERTISING DIRECTLY:
  "Glamorization, of course, is starkly obvious in advertising. Here we see
  it sui generis. The cowboy is the glamorization of masculinity, and the
  ballet dancer is the glamorization of femininity. Men are attracted by
  personality, not the brand; thus, the cowboy represents the glamorized
  male who is rugged, cool, suave, and in control. The consumer projects onto
  the product that it will give him those desired personality traits."

  "If you let go of the romanticization of the cowboy, for example, then the
  cigarette or the cheeseburger that he was handling in the commercial will
  lose its appeal."

  "much to our surprise, we will find over and over again that the desire
  was attached to the glamorous fantasy; there was no reality in it in the
  first place. Because there was no reality in it, the world is constantly
  selling us dishonesty, catering to our desire for that romantic,
  glamorized aspect. It promises to make us more important than we really
  are. Glamour at that level of dishonesty is a fake."

What people really want underneath the glamour:
  "We can be attractive, but we won't get it in a fake way such as driving a
  certain style of car. We will get it by letting go of our smallness and
  owning our greatness... We can easily become that exciting person whom
  people are eager to know."

7.4 People buy the feeling, not the thing  [Ch. 3, 7, 17]
---------------------------------------------------------
Together, the "What for?" method, the glamour section, and the money section
make one point: people do not want the object. They want the FEELING they
think the object will give them, which is usually security, respect,
approval, importance, attractiveness, or happiness.

"No amount of riches can compensate for an inner feeling of poverty."  [Ch. 3]

"the smaller we feel inside, the greater amount of power, money, and glamour
must be accumulated in order to try and compensate for the inner
smallness."  [Ch. 17]

The man worth $50 million who panicked over losing $10 million was
"frightened that he would not be able to survive on the planet with only 40
million dollars."  [Ch. 17]

7.5 Questions that de-glamorize  [Ch. 17]
-----------------------------------------
  "Is it really all that money that I want, or is it the glamour that I
  have attached to it? What is it that I want from that job title...? Is it
  the responsibility and activities that go along with it, or is it the
  glamour and esteem associated with it? Do I really love that person, or am
  I in love with the glamour I have projected onto him or her?"

  "The more we let go, the more we de-glamorize the world. The more it is
  de-glamorized, the less it runs us... We are no longer vulnerable to the
  professional programmers of the media and the political and social
  arenas."

7.6 Attachment and "mine"  [Ch. 5, Ch. 9]
-----------------------------------------
  - Things that meet an inner need become "mine" and then part of "me."
    [Ch. 5]
  - "It is a comical curiosity of the human mind to watch how it attaches
    pride to anything that is prefixed by the word 'mine.'"  [Ch. 9]
  - Thrift-store example: "an article of clothing hanging in a thrift shop
    has no value at all until it becomes 'mine.' Instantly, great value is
    attached to it."  [Ch. 9]
  - "Possessiveness and attachment occur as a consequence of pride."  [Ch. 9]

7.7 Selfishness: the small self's operating mode  [Ch. 7]
---------------------------------------------------------
Hawkins says "selfishness" names the "collective motivations and modes of
operation of the small self." The reason to drop it is not guilt but
practicality: "it is impractical. It doesn't work. It's too costly. It
consumes too much energy."

Moving from small-s "selfish" to capital-S "Selfish" means moving "from
weakness to power... from strife to ease, and from frustration to
accomplishment."


===============================================================================
PART 8 - PRIDE, LABELS, OPINIONS, AND EXPLOITABILITY
===============================================================================
[Ch. 9 - "Pride"]

8.1 Pride is a cover for insecurity
-----------------------------------
"The smaller we feel within, the more we have to compensate for an inner
sense of inadequacy, unimportance, and valuelessness by the substitution of
the emotion of pride."

"Many people try to substitute pride for genuine self-esteem; however,
genuine self-esteem does not actually arise until pride is relinquished."

"We puff up with pride whenever we feel insecure."  [Ch. 18]

8.2 Pride is vulnerable and invites attack
------------------------------------------
"The prideful person is constantly on the defensive." Their energy goes to
"defending our lifestyle, vocation, neighborhood, clothes, year and make of
car, ancestry, country, and political and religious belief systems. There is
a tireless preoccupation with appearance and what other people will think."

LAW: "Defensiveness invites attack."

"There is a subtle inference of apology in pride, as though the thing in
itself was not good enough to stand on its own merit."

8.3 Status symbols and labels
-----------------------------
"We can recognize our proneness to pain by looking at the kind of reactions
we are hoping to elicit from others by our choices and behavior. This
includes mannerisms, expressions, style of dress, the kind of possessions we
choose, the brand name of car we drive, the kind of home we have, the
address at which we live, the schools we have attended or which our children
attend, or the labels on the products we buy."

"Labels are now worn on the outside of many clothes and personal articles."

8.4 Pride makes people easy to manipulate
-----------------------------------------
"This points to another one of the drawbacks of pride: the exploitability to
which it exposes us. Pridefulness means that we can be manipulated with
great ease. In return for an absurdity, a great deal of money is lifted out
of our pocketbooks."

"It is a current status symbol among certain circles to brag about how much
one has paid for certain things."

Reverse snobbery: some people take pride in "bargains" and thrift-store
finds. "In this thrift store snobbery crowd, the status symbol is the
incredible bargain."

8.5 Ostentation does not truly impress
--------------------------------------
"Does ostentation really impress? Actually, no. The response that we see is
one of fascination. People get a charge out of the superficial glamour, but
underneath they do not truly respect it."

The contrast case: a woman worth millions who "represents absolute
simplicity." She "takes joy in their beauty. Consequently, never a single
criticism is made of her, nor do others express envy." She has no burglar
alarms.

"It is not what we have that matters, but how we hold it."

"Pride, like all the other negative emotions, engenders guilt. Guilt
engenders fear. Fear means potential loss. Pride, therefore, always means a
loss of peace of mind."

8.6 Antidotes: gratitude, joy, love, simplicity
-----------------------------------------------
  - "Gratitude is one of the antidotes of pride."
  - Joy as a better motivator than pride: "If we achieve a certain goal for
    the pleasure, enjoyment, love of accomplishment, and the inner joy that
    it brings to us, we are invulnerable to the reaction of others."
  - "The opposite of prideful acquisitiveness is simplicity... it is a
    state of mind."
  - Love is higher than pride: "Our sheer love and appreciation for
    something is a solid position that cannot be assailed."

8.7 Opinions
------------
"opinions... are a dime a dozen." They change "from moment to moment and are
vulnerable to every passing whim of fashion, propaganda, and faddism."

"If we look back on our life, we will see that every mistake we ever made
was based on an opinion."

"it is primarily our emotions that are giving them any value in the first
place."

Saying "I do this because I enjoy it" disarms people. Saying "I do this
because it's right" makes them bristle: "we will instantly see their hackles
go up because they, also, have an opinion on what is right."

"Our values are preferences. We hold them because we love them, enjoy them,
and get pleasure from them."

The dieter example: people who are prideful about their diet argue
constantly. People who follow the same diet "because they enjoy doing so...
are never heard to be in argument." "If... they infer that theirs is the
right way... what they are really saying is that they are better than we
are. That always arouses resentment."

8.8 Pride locks people into bad positions
-----------------------------------------
"How often have we gotten stuck in performing something we really didn't
want to do, because we had foolishly taken a prideful stance on an opinion!"
A resistance to changing positions is "fear... that, if we change our
position in a certain matter, the opinion of others about us will be
adversely affected."

"Pride prevents us from recognizing our own limitations and accepting the
help we need to overcome them." Addicts may die because of the pride-driven
denial: "Other people have the problem - not me!"

"When we let go of pride, help comes into our life."

8.9 False modesty and false humility
------------------------------------
"False modesty is the pretense of self-diminishment with the longing that
others will recognize the accomplishments that one is so proud of, but too
proud to brag about openly."  [Ch. 9]

"False humility merely says to the other person, 'I am a small person;
please treat me that way' and, of course, they promptly do."  [Ch. 18]

"When we stop wanting to be liked, we find that we are. When we stop catering
to others and trying to manipulate their approval, we find that they do
respect us."  [Ch. 18]


===============================================================================
PART 9 - FEAR, GUILT, AND PROGRAMMING
===============================================================================

9.1 The mind as a computer that gets programmed  [Ch. 4, Ch. 6]
---------------------------------------------------------------
"We can look at the mind's concepts, thoughts, and belief systems as
programs. Because they are programs, they can be questioned, cancelled, and
reversed."  [Ch. 4]

Where programs come from: "early childhood training from parents, family,
and teachers, as well as dribbles and drabbles of information we picked up
from playmates, newspapers, movies, television, radio, church, novels... All
of this went on unwittingly without our having exercised any conscious
choice."  [Ch. 4]

"Is it not so that we have bought into ten thousand lies and are willing to
buy another ten thousand out of the naivete of our inner innocence? Is not
that inner innocence the very reason for our exploitability?"  [Ch. 6]

9.2 Hawkins' view of how the world uses fear and guilt  [Ch. 6]
---------------------------------------------------------------
Hawkins sees much of the world's messaging as "an attempt by others to
control us; exploit us; extract our money, our services, our energy, our
loyalties; and capture our mind."

"It's time to re-own our own power and stop giving it away to every passing
scammer who jiggles our fears and shakes loose some money out of our
pocketbook."

"When we become aware of how we were manipulated, exploited, and taken in,
anger is going to come up."  [Ch. 4]

"We have allowed ourselves to be manipulated by guilt, and we turn it around
and use that same mechanism of guilt to try to exploit and control others."
[Ch. 6]

"When it comes to healing ourselves... it 'pays to be paranoid.' We become
aware of all the guilt-mongers in our life."  [Ch. 6]

9.3 Fear sells, but at a cost  [Ch. 6]
--------------------------------------
"the media present us over and over again with fearful situations, as in
this morning's news: 'Terrorist group threatens to poison our food supply.'
Such headlines are constant."

The patient Betty "believed every scare story she read" until she was afraid
of nearly everything: food, air, sunlight, dyes, germs.

Preoccupation with health fears produces "the endless reading of labels for
potentially poisonous ingredients" and "an obsession with offsetting all of
these 'dangers' by various countermeasures."  [Ch. 17]

When a public figure announces an illness, "there is a sudden jump in the
incidence of that illness."  [Ch. 17]

9.4 Doing the right thing from love instead of fear  [Ch. 6]
------------------------------------------------------------
"We could take the same protective actions out of love rather than out of
fear. Can we not care for our bodies because we appreciate and value them,
rather than out of fear of disease and dying?... Can we not do a good job
because we care about the quality of our performance... rather than just the
fear of losing our jobs?... Can we not accomplish more by cooperation,
rather than by fearful competition? Can we not drive carefully because we
have a high regard for ourselves... rather than because we fear an
accident?"

"Could we not achieve the same motivation or behavior out of love rather
than out of fear and guilt?"

9.5 The shadow and why violent entertainment attracts  [Ch. 6]
--------------------------------------------------------------
"What makes the endless television programs, which are concerned with
mayhem and its various forms, so attractive? It is because what is being
acted out on the screen, where it is safe, are all the forbidden unconscious
fantasies in our own psyche."

"Everybody secretly harbors the fear that they are dumb, ugly, unlovable, and
a failure."

9.6 Fear of others' opinions  [Ch. 6]
-------------------------------------
"One of the blocks to becoming acquainted with the fears in one's own mind is
the fear of the opinions of others. The wanting of their approval goes on
inside of our minds in a constant fantasy. We identify with the opinions of
others, including authority figures, and coalesce this in such a way that we
hear it as our own opinion of ourselves."

9.7 The crabs in a bucket  [Ch. 6]
----------------------------------
"The degree to which we have not allowed ourselves to experience the reality
of our true Self is represented by our resentment toward those who have
actually done so. We resent their aliveness in the areas in which we feel
disabled." The fisherman needs no lid on his crab pail because "as one crab
crawls up the side of the pail to get out, the other crabs reach up and grab
him and pull him back down."

9.8 Envy  [Ch. 19]
------------------
"the other person's achievement triggers in us a feeling of lack or
inadequacy about ourselves... 'Maybe I'm not accomplishing what I should be
accomplishing.'" Then "we often resent the person whose successes have
inadvertently provoked this feeling." The Chariots of Fire runner who looked
back to compare himself to a competitor lost the race. The winner "ran his
best because he loved to run."


===============================================================================
PART 10 - HOW AND WHY WE MAKE DECISIONS
===============================================================================

10.1 Decisions are made by feelings, then justified by logic  [Ch. 3]
---------------------------------------------------------------------
(See Part 2.) Reason serves emotion. People "develop all kinds of plausible
reasons" and "frequently do not know why they are doing what they are
doing."

10.2 Why we regret decisions  [Ch. 16 - "Problem Solving"]
----------------------------------------------------------
"Think of how often we have changed our mind and regretted past decisions.
That is because there was an unrecognized and un-relinquished feeling behind
the decision. When the action that was decided upon is taken, the underlying
feeling shifts. Then, from the viewpoint of the new feeling space, the
decision turns out to be wrong. This happens with such regularity that most
people develop a fear of decision-making."

"When we first clear out the underlying feelings, the decisions are more
realistic and wise."

10.3 Problem solving: look at the feeling, not the answer  [Ch. 16]
-------------------------------------------------------------------
"Don't look for answers; instead, let go of the feelings behind the
question." "The reason the mind can't decide is because it is looking in the
wrong place."

Example: a couple arguing about which movie to see. The real feeling was
resentment about too little romantic time. Once that came out, they didn't
want a movie at all. Or the movie was a way to avoid closeness out of fear.

The passport example [Ch. 3]: a man frantically searched for a lost
passport. When he let go of the hidden grief about leaving a loved one, "he
instantly remembered where the passport was."

10.4 The three decision states  [Ch. 19 - "Feelings and the Decision-Making
     Process"]
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Hawkins simplifies the scale into three states for decision-making:

  STATE 1: INERT  (Apathy, Grief, Fear)
  - Feelings pull attention onto "our own thoughts, most of which are in the
    realm of 'I don't know,' 'I'm not sure,' and 'I don't think I can.'"
  - This renders us "temporarily unable to perceive the full dimension and
    possibilities of the overall situation."
  - People stall decisions "until we feel better," or make a decision that
    "is not sustainable in the long run because it is based on the feeling
    state, and when the feeling state changes, the decision has to be
    changed with it."
  - Result: "inner insecurity, ambivalence, confusion, and a loss of
    confidence in us by those around us." "Garbage in and garbage out."

  STATE 2: ENERGETIC  (Desire, Anger, Pride)
  - "This is the state of the 'go-getter.'"
  - Some positive thoughts flow through, so things get done, but with
    "unevenness of performance."
  - Driven by "ambition, desire, or 'proving oneself'"; decisions can be
    "compulsive or impulsive."
  - "personal self-gain as the primary motivating factor."
  - Decisions are often unsustainable because they are "win-lose" rather than
    "win-win."
  - Called "solar plexus" motivation: "self-centered and driven by personal
    motives, with little concern for the welfare of others."

  STATE 3: PEACEFUL  (Courage, Acceptance, Love)
  - These feelings "allow us to concentrate completely on the situation and
    observe all of the relevant details."
  - "inspiration brings forth ideas that solve the problem."
  - Solutions are win-win: "because everyone benefits, everyone lends their
    energy to the project and success is shared by all."
  - "when everyone's needs in a situation are met, our own needs are
    fulfilled automatically."
  - "If we look at a situation and claim that a win-win solution is not
    possible, that should warn us that we have some un-surrendered inner
    feelings blocking a possibly perfect solution."

10.5 Choice, intention, and decision  [Ch. 7, Ch. 18]
-----------------------------------------------------
"The majority of what happens in our lifetime is the result of some decision
we have made somewhere in the past, either consciously or unconsciously."
[Ch. 7]

"events are due to decisions, and choices are based on our intentions. What
we get is the result of these choices, even though they are unconscious,
rather than what we think we want. When we surrender the pressure of
wantingness, we are clear to make wiser choices and decisions."  [Ch. 18]

Hawkins' recommended way to pursue something: "by envisioning what we wish
to have happen. We do this by declaration of our intention, by acceptance,
by decision, and by the act of consciously choosing."  [Ch. 7]

10.6 Fear of making a decision  [Ch. 6, Ch. 16]
-----------------------------------------------
"fear of making a decision" appears in the book's list of common fears. Many
people develop it because past decisions so often "turned out to be wrong"
once the hidden feeling shifted.

10.7 Facts vs. feelings about facts  [Ch. 3, Ch. 18]
----------------------------------------------------
"It is not the facts that we fear but our feelings about them."  [Ch. 3]

"We think that our happiness depends on controlling events, and that facts
are what upset us. Actually, it is our feelings and thoughts about these
facts that are the real cause of our upset. Facts in and of themselves are
neutral things."  [Ch. 18]

10.8 Willingness to take risks rises with emotional mastery  [Ch. 3]
--------------------------------------------------------------------
"Fear of life is really the fear of emotions... Once we have mastery over our
feelings, our fear of life diminishes. We feel a greater self-confidence, and
we are willing to take greater chances because we now feel that we can
handle the emotional consequences, whatever they might be. Because fear is
the basis of all inhibitions, mastery over fear means the unblocking of
whole avenues of life experience that previously had been avoided."

10.9 "I can't" usually means "I won't"  [Ch. 4]
-----------------------------------------------
When someone says they can't (dance, speak, spend), Hawkins suggests imagining
actually doing it. The feelings that come up show the real block:
"embarrassment, pride, awkwardness, the sheer effort of learning a new skill,
and the reluctance about the time and energy involved." The questions:
  "Would I be willing to let go of the fear of not succeeding? Would I be
  willing to let go of resisting the effort required? Would I be willing to
  let go of the vanity so that I could allow myself to be awkward as a
  learner? Could I let go of my stinginess and smallness and be willing to
  pay for the lessons and give the time?"
"the real reason is unwillingness - not incapacity."

PART 11 - INFLUENCE, PRESSURE, RESISTANCE, AND SELLING
===============================================================================

11.1 Pressure creates resistance  [Ch. 8, Ch. 18]
-------------------------------------------------
"That which we want, desire, and insist upon from another person is felt by
them as pressure. They will, therefore, unconsciously resist... pressure is
always felt by us as a denial of our choice. It is felt as emotional
blackmail."  [Ch. 8]

"We all know the resistance we feel when we become aware that somebody is
fishing for a compliment."  [Ch. 8]

"When we put pressure on other people in order to get what we want, they
automatically resist... The harder we push, the harder they resist. Even
though, out of fear, they may actually concede to our demands, there is not
an inner acceptance and, later on, we will lose what we have gained."
[Ch. 18]

"Emotions are really subtle attempts to force others and impose our will on
them, which they unconsciously resist."  [Ch. 18]

"Because people intuitively pick up our wish to control them, their response
is to resist. So the only way to bring about relinquishment of their
resisting us is to let go of wanting to influence them in the first place."
[Ch. 18]

11.2 Expectation is emotional blackmail  [Ch. 8]
------------------------------------------------
"Expectation of others is a form of emotional blackmail. We can feel our
resistance when others pull for certain emotional 'goods' from us."

"When we stop pressuring others with our expectations, we create an opening
for them spontaneously to respond positively to us."

"A forced compliment does not satisfy."

11.3 The boy/girl theory (Robert Ringer)  [Ch. 8, Ch. 18]
---------------------------------------------------------
"Boy wants girl, therefore, girl is disinterested in boy. As soon as boy is no
longer interested in girl, girl now wants boy."

Hawkins applies it to sales: "When we refer to this phenomenon to explain
sales resistance, one way around it is to take the view that our
responsibility is to make an effort, but not try to determine the result.
Another way is to surrender the feelings we have about what we want from the
other person, and let go of the pressures we are putting on them in the form
of expectation and desire. They, then, have the psychic space to become
agreeable or even to initiate the desired result on their own."  [Ch. 18]

THE SHIRT EXPERIMENT [Ch. 8]: A man wore a new Mexican shirt while keeping
his pride and expectation of being noticed. Nobody said a word. The next day
he wore the other one after letting go of all expectation, and "Almost every
person he met commented on the new shirt." Lesson: "We get what we want when
we stop insisting on it!"

THE DIVORCE EXAMPLE [Ch. 18]: His wife kept saying "No" to something he
wanted. When he became okay either way, "she suddenly turned to him and
offered, not only to give it to him, but to have it packed and shipped as
well."

11.4 Surrendered does not mean passive  [Ch. 18, Ch. 21]
--------------------------------------------------------
"The way to facilitate satisfaction in relationships is lovingly to picture
the best possible outcome. Make sure it is mutually beneficial: a win-win
situation... We can tell if we are really surrendered when we feel okay
either way... Therefore, to be surrendered does not mean to be passive. It is
being active in a positive way."  [Ch. 18]

"surrendering will clear the decks for effective action."  [Ch. 21]

"Patience is an automatic side effect of letting go... Notice that patient
people usually get what they want in the end."  [Ch. 18]

11.5 FEELINGS AND SALES ABILITY  [Ch. 19 - quoted and summarized closely]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
Hawkins applies his three states directly to selling "a product or ideas or
our personal services."

  INERT SALESPERSON (Apathy, Grief, Fear)
  - "sales ability is at its lowest."
  - Prospect says they're not interested -> "They don't want my product."
  - Escapes into coffee breaks and chat; concentration and resourcefulness
    drop.
  - "The low self-esteem creates vulnerability to discouragement, which, in
    turn, creates expectations of failure. When thoughts of failure are held
    in mind, failure is precipitated in the sales situation."

  ENERGETIC SALESPERSON (Desire, Anger, Pride)
  - "a greater degree of vigor and drive."
  - "excessive verbalization, with more time spent talking to sales prospects
    than listening to them."
  - "premature closings, pushing too hard."
  - Goals can be met here, but the block is "the focus on self-gain and the
    underlying viewpoint, 'I win; they lose.' This self-seeking motive is
    intuitively picked up by sales prospects and may lead to resistance."
  - Typical thought: "I want to get them to buy it, so I'll get a good
    commission."

  PEACEFUL SALESPERSON (Courage, Acceptance, Love)
  - "our ability to concentrate is at its highest."
  - "able to listen carefully to the other person, and place the sale in a
    context of what is beneficial to the buyer rather than to the seller."
  - "never at a loss for creative ideas as to what will produce sales or what
    will turn problems into solutions."
  - "often converts customers to friends, and the customers tend to become
    loyal."
  - "Achievement of sales goals on this level is assured because what is
    being held in mind is the positive situation of win-win."

Evidence Hawkins cites:
  - The art gallery worker who hadn't sold in weeks despite "visualization,
    positive thinking, advance sales techniques, and written affirmations."
    She finally let go of all the striving and sold two copies of a
    sculpture, titled "Letting Go," within the first hour.
  - A study at a large American insurance company: within six months of
    learning the technique, agents' sales rose "by 33% over a control
    group." The study's author concluded "success in the world is related to
    our ability to concentrate."

Hawkins' summary of the most successful people:
  "The most successful people in the world are those who hold in mind the
  highest good of all concerned, including themselves. They know that there
  is a win-win solution to every problem... They do work which they love...
  They do not seek happiness; they have discovered that happiness is a
  by-product of doing what they love."

11.6 Win-lose vs. win-win  [Ch. 18]
-----------------------------------
"While the intimidators may seem to get away with something temporarily on
the surface, they really have lost the whole thing... What we win by the
negative emotion is short-lived and inauthentic. It doesn't really satisfy.
It's like a forced compliment. Real happiness results from a win-win
situation. The price of a win-lose situation is hatred and low self-esteem.
Underneath it all, we fool neither ourselves, nor others. Others always know
when we are out to exploit them."

11.7 Acknowledgment: a secret of relationship  [Ch. 8]
------------------------------------------------------
"One of the great secrets of relationship is acknowledgment."

"everyone feels pleased when we acknowledge their value." Thanking friends
for calling "makes the other persons feel complete and secure with us."

"By this simple mechanism of acknowledgment, it is possible, within a matter
of days, to transform all of one's relationships in a rather dramatic way."

Unacknowledged care is a major source of quiet anger: "There are people...
in our life who are walking around with an endless mental stream of thoughts
about us, having to do with our lack of appreciation of their feelings for
us."

"Once their value to us has been acknowledged, the nagging stops."

11.8 Presence and attraction  [Ch. 4, 6, 7, 16, 18]
---------------------------------------------------
  - People are drawn to high-energy people: "We tend to seek out such
    people."  [Ch. 3]
  - When the man stopped grasping, "Waitresses and cab drivers suddenly
    mysteriously become attentive and courteous... 'What has come over this
    world?' The answer to that question is 'You have!'"  [Ch. 16]
  - At the level of being, people seek you "because of what we have
    become."  [Ch. 7]
  - "Love is automatically attracted to the person who is loving."  [Ch. 5]
  - The overweight woman who had felt invisible: "I was giving love instead
    of looking for it... I used to scare them away because I was so hungry...
    Now I feel full."  [Ch. 18]
  - "when we seek to give instead of to get, all of our own needs are
    automatically fulfilled."  [Ch. 18]

11.9 Humor and shared humanness  [Ch. 6]
----------------------------------------
Hawkins began speeches by announcing, "I am one of the most boring speakers
around." "Surprisingly, this brought a laugh from the audience. Their
laughter signified acceptance of our common humanness, and so the fears were
lifted."

"humor is valuable in public speaking. It is a way of just being at one with
the humanness of the audience and discovering their compassion... We love
them for relieving our fear and accepting us, and they love us back for
doing the very thing that they themselves fear."

11.10 Affirmations and the "Yeah, but..."  [Ch. 21]
---------------------------------------------------
"An affirmation is a positive statement. Its power is limited by the fact
that... we have multiple negative programs that are saying the very opposite
thing... your mind comes up with, 'Yeah, but...' It is these 'Yeah, buts'
that limit the power of the affirmation."

11.11 Skepticism  [Ch. 1]
-------------------------
"It's okay to be skeptical. We've been taken down the primrose path before...
it's advisable to avoid gushing enthusiasm. It is a setup for a letdown
later. Therefore, rather than enthusiasm, quiet observation will serve you
better."

Hawkins on the boss and promotion: "Why is it... that the other person gets
the promotion or the recognition, and we do not? The answer is to look at our
hidden inner feelings about the boss and the job. Do we really think that he
hasn't registered our inner envy, criticalness, and resentment?"

Exercise Hawkins gives: "First, look at how you are secretly feeling about a
person in a given situation. Presume that the other person is aware of those
thoughts and feelings. Then, put yourself in their place and see how you

reward."

attachment, dependency, and possessiveness."  [Ch. 18]

"If their happiness were best served by leaving me, how would I feel about

PART 13 - [APPLICATION - NOT FROM THE BOOK]
           TRANSLATING HAWKINS' PSYCHOLOGY INTO ADVERTISING
===============================================================================

IMPORTANT: Everything in this section is my interpretation and application of
the book's ideas to advertising. Hawkins did not write this. Each point names
the part of the book it comes from so you can judge it.

One honest caution first. By Hawkins' own account, the classic ad toolkit of
glamour, fear, guilt, status, and pressure works at the levels BELOW 200. He
calls these "force," and he says they produce resistance, short-lived
results, buyer's remorse, and the feeling of being exploited ("Others always
know when we are out to exploit them"). If you want to follow his psychology
and not just borrow it, your ads should come from Courage, Acceptance, and
Love. The principles below are written with that in mind.

13.1 Sell the feeling, then show the truth of the thing
  [Parts 1.4, 2.4, 7.3, 7.4]
  - Use "What for?" on your customer. Ask why they want your product, then
    keep asking until you reach the real feeling: security, respect,
    belonging, ease, happiness, or freedom from a fear.
  - Speak to that feeling. But Hawkins warns that glamour, meaning a thing
    made "bigger than life," leads to disillusionment. Promise only the
    feeling the product can actually deliver. That avoids the designer-dress
    letdown and the refunds and bad reviews that follow it.

13.2 Meet the audience where they are, then invite them one step up
  [Parts 5, 6.1]
  - Know which level your audience is at about the problem you solve:
      Apathy  "I can't / nothing works"
      Grief   "if only... someone help me"
      Fear    "I'm afraid of X"
      Desire  "I want X now"
      Anger   "I'm fed up with X"
      Pride   "I deserve the best / I'm better than this"
      Courage "I'm ready to handle this"
  - The ladder in Ch. 4 suggests an ad works best when it moves people ONE
    rung up, not when it shouts from the top. Examples: turn "I can't" into
    "you're not incapable, you just haven't had the right tool." Turn fear
    into courage: "you can handle this."
  - "I can't" usually means "I won't" (Part 10.9). An ad can name the real
    hidden objection, such as embarrassment, the effort of learning, cost,
    or fear of looking awkward, and remove it.

13.3 Make COURAGE the emotional center of the call to action
  [Parts 5.2 Courage, 6.1, 10.8]
  - Courage (200) is where action becomes possible: "I can do it." Hawkins
    says it's "easy to jump from any of the lower feelings up to courage
    merely by affirming our courage." A call to action that says "you can
    handle this" applies that directly.
  - People will take chances once they feel they can handle the emotional
    consequences. Guarantees, easy returns, free trials, and "nothing to
    lose" lower the emotional cost of a decision.

13.4 Remove pressure: the boy/girl theory and the shirt experiment
  [Part 11.1-11.4]
  - Hawkins: pressure is "felt as emotional blackmail" and produces
    resistance. Even when it works, "later on, we will lose what we have
    gained."
  - Ads that feel needy, desperate, or pushy (fake countdown timers, guilt
    copy, "you'd be stupid not to") trigger this resistance.
  - The non-needy stance ("it's fine either way") is calm and confident.
    Present the offer clearly, show the benefit, and leave the choice with
    the buyer. "Pressure is always felt by us as a denial of our choice," so
    an ad that respects choice lowers resistance.

13.5 Write from the "Peaceful Salesperson" stance
  [Part 11.5]
  - Listen more than you talk. In ads, that means copy built on the
    customer's actual words, objections, and situation, not a list of
    features.
  - Frame the purchase as "beneficial to the buyer rather than to the
    seller."
  - Aim for customers who become "friends" and stay "loyal." Repeat buyers
    and referrals come from win-win, not win-lose.
  - The "I win, they lose" motive, "intuitively picked up by sales
    prospects," shows up in copy as hype, exaggeration, and manufactured
    urgency.

13.6 Use the having -> doing -> being progression in messaging
  [Part 6.7]
  - Lower-level messaging is about HAVING ("own this").
  - Middle-level messaging is about DOING ("achieve this," "be productive").
  - Higher-level messaging is about BEING ("become the person who..."). It
    is the most powerful, by Hawkins' account, because "the level of being
    has the most power and energy."
  - Hawkins also says the real route to being attractive is "letting go of
    our smallness and owning our greatness," not "driving a certain style of
    car." An ad that tells people the product supports who they already are,
    and does not create who they are, stays honest.

13.7 Status and pride: understand them, but be careful
  [Part 8]
  - Pride is a cover for insecurity, and status buyers are buying relief
    from feeling small. Hawkins says this makes them "manipulated with great
    ease," but also that ostentation produces "fascination," not respect,
    and that pride brings defensiveness, envy, and fear of loss.
  - The higher alternative he names is JOY and GRATITUDE in ownership:
    "we enjoy its beauty and perfection and we feel grateful for having it."
    For premium products, sell beauty, craftsmanship, enjoyment, and
    gratitude, not superiority over others.
  - Avoid "better than you" framing. Per the dieter example, "That always
    arouses resentment." "Love it because you enjoy it" invites no
    argument.

13.8 Fear and guilt: the book's strongest warnings
  [Parts 1.5, 9.2-9.4]
  - Fear is "the predominant ruling emotion of our world," so fear-based ads
    will get attention. But Hawkins calls the people who exploit this
    "scammers who jiggle our fears" and "guilt-mongers." When audiences
    realize they've been manipulated, "anger is going to come up."
  - His alternative is the same protective action, motivated by love instead
    of fear: "Can we not care for our bodies because we appreciate and value
    them, rather than out of fear of disease and dying?"
    Ad translation: "Protect what you love" and "care for yourself because
    you're worth it" instead of "you'll regret it if you don't."
  - Guilt copy ("you should be doing better") lands on the person's built-in
    sense that "we 'should' be doing something else." It may convert, but
    it attaches your brand to self-condemnation.

13.9 Acknowledge your customer
  [Part 11.7]
  - "everyone feels pleased when we acknowledge their value." Ads, emails,
    and post-purchase messages that genuinely acknowledge the customer's
    effort, intelligence, or situation make people feel "complete and
    secure with us."
  - Thank-you messages, recognizing loyal customers, and naming their
    struggle respectfully all apply this.

13.10 Humor and shared humanness
  [Part 11.9]
  - Self-deprecating honesty ("I am one of the most boring speakers
    around") disarmed Hawkins' audience: "Their laughter signified
    acceptance of our common humanness." Brands that admit a small flaw with
    humor can lower defenses.

13.11 Social proof as "the company we keep"
  [Part 6.6]
  - People rise when around "the winners" who have solved their problem.
    Testimonials from people who were once where the prospect is now, and
    got out, fit this exactly. Show the before (their current level) and
    the after (one or more levels up).

13.12 Reduce decision regret
  [Part 10.2]
  - Hawkins: decisions made from a passing feeling get reversed "when the
    underlying feeling shifts." Hype-driven purchases are the classic case.
  - Ads that create calm clarity (clear benefits, honest expectations, no
    artificial urgency) produce decisions that hold, which means fewer
    refunds and more trust.

13.13 Handle skepticism directly
  [Part 7.2, 11.11]
  - Audiences already believe "There's got to be a catch," "It's too good to
    be true," "It can happen for others but not for me."
  - Hawkins' own approach is to say "It's okay to be skeptical" and
    recommend "quiet observation" over "gushing enthusiasm." Copy that
    invites the reader to test the claim for themselves borrows that
    approach.

13.14 Quick checklist to test an ad against the book
  [ ] What FEELING does the buyer actually want? (What for? x4)
  [ ] What LEVEL is the buyer at about this problem?
  [ ] Does the ad lift them at least one level, ideally to Courage?
  [ ] Is there any glamour that promises more than the product delivers?
  [ ] Does it use fear or guilt where love or care would do the same job?
  [ ] Does it feel needy or pushy? Would it still work if "it's okay either
      way"?
  [ ] Is it win-win? Would the buyer feel good about it a month later?
  [ ] Does it acknowledge the buyer's value?
  [ ] Does it respect the buyer's freedom to choose?

E. THE BOOK'S MOST USEFUL SELF-INQUIRY QUESTIONS
------------------------------------------------
  - "What for?" (repeat until the core feeling appears)  [Ch. 3]
  - "What is the basic feeling that I've been ignoring?"  [Ch. 3]
  - "Is it true that I won't rather than that I can't?"  [Ch. 4]
  - "What am I trying to prove? How much am I willing to pay to be
    'right'?"  [Ch. 4]
  - "What purpose is [this person/object] serving in my life?"  [Ch. 5]
  - "Where am I looking to get love rather than to give it?"  [Ch. 5]
  - "What's the worst possible thing that could happen?"  [Ch. 6]
  - "What is the purpose of pride? What is its payoff? For what does it
    compensate?"  [Ch. 9]
  - "What have I failed to acknowledge in those with whom I have daily
    contact?"  [Ch. 8]
  - "Is it really all that money that I want, or is it the glamour that I
    have attached to it?"  [Ch. 17]
  - "How would I react if I were the other person and knew exactly what my
    personal inner feelings and thoughts really were?"  [Ch. 18]
  - "If this were the last day of our life, would that really be what we
    wanted?"  [Ch. 18]
