# Static Image Ads: Window Cleaning & Soft Washing

**For:** TideWater Window Cleaning, serving all of Albemarle County, VA (most customers in Charlottesville, Crozet and Glenmore)
**Buyer:** Homeowners 45 to 85+, mostly women. Lots of windows, little time, can't reach the outsides. Trust and quality matter more than price. Local first.
**Built from:** the avatar sheet plus these files in this repo: `$100M Hooks`, `$100M GOATed Ads`, `ACQ Advertising Handbook` (20 frameworks + Ad Kaleidoscope), `100M Leads` (paid ads chapter), `$100M Proof Checklist`, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (headlines + illustrations), *Breakthrough Advertising* (awareness + sophistication), `Hooks_and_Mechanisms_Chris_Haddad`, `THE DIRECT RESPONSE HEADLINE ENGINE`, `Hawkins_Human_Psychology_Emotions_Decisions` (Map of Consciousness), `$100M Branding`.

Anything in `[brackets]` is a placeholder for you to fill in with your real numbers, names, dates and neighborhoods.

## The 10 rules every idea was built on

1. **In a static ad, the photo and headline are the whole hook.** Spend 80% of your effort there. If they don't stop, nothing else matters. (`$100M Hooks`, `GOATed Ads`)
2. **Call out place + person.** "LOCAL AREA + TYPE OF PERSON" is Hormozi's all-time favorite local callout. The more local, the better: Virginians < Charlottesville < Crozet. Ogilvy found local ads do better with the town name in the headline. (`100M Leads`, Ogilvy)
3. **Show, don't tell.** Gallup studied 70 campaigns and did not find one before-and-after campaign that failed to increase sales. Proof beats claims. (Ogilvy, `Proof Checklist`)
4. **Story appeal.** The best photos make people ask, "What's going on here?" and then read to find out. (Ogilvy)
5. **Look like the buyer.** Show homeowners who look like your customers and your young, friendly crew (the "boy next door"). Keep one person or thing in focus, not crowds, and don't blow faces up bigger than life. (`100M Leads` likeness, Ogilvy)
6. **Other people saying it beats you saying it.** Raw beats polished. Newer beats older. Numbers beat no numbers. Local beats far away. (`Proof Checklist`)
7. **Specific beats vague. Simple beats clever.** Ogilvy found headlines in quote marks get 28% more recall, and "blind" or punny headlines do worse. (Ogilvy, `Headline Engine`)
8. **Spread ideas across all five awareness levels.** Curiosity for the unaware, pain for the problem-aware, promise for the solution-aware, proof for the product-aware, offer for the most aware. (`Breakthrough Advertising`, `GOATed Ads`)
9. **Clear beats clever on the CTA.** Say what to do, when, what they get, and what happens next. (`GOATed Ads`)
10. **Make lots, then multiply the winners.** Find the 2 or 3 that win, then run them through the Ad Kaleidoscope (new headline, background, border, crop, neighborhood, person) instead of starting from scratch. (`ACQ Advertising Handbook`)
11. **Lift them one level.** Start where the buyer is emotionally (guilt, apathy, fear, frustration, pride), then raise them a step toward courage, acceptance or love. Fear and guilt get attention, but on their own they cause resistance and regret. (Hawkins, Map of Consciousness)
12. **Sell who they are.** People buy roles, not just results: the particular homeowner, the good neighbor, the one who takes care of what's theirs. Make hiring you a sign of who they already are. (*Breakthrough Advertising*, Hawkins)

**Awareness tags used below:** `U` Unaware · `P` Problem-aware · `S` Solution-aware · `Pr` Product-aware · `M` Most aware

## Before you run any of these

- **Use real photos, real reviews and real quotes, with the customer's OK.** Never make up a review, a text message or a "neighbor's post." It breaks FTC and Meta rules, and real proof works better anyway (raw > processed).
- **Meta won't let ads say or imply someone's age, health or similar personal traits.** "Are you over 70?" or "Widowed and living alone?" will get rejected. Use a customer quote ("I'm in my eighties, and the job is just overwhelming") or talk about the house instead.
- **Only claim what's true.** Review counts, star ratings, guarantees, plant protection, soft wash pressure, insurance, neighbors booked: fill these in only if they're accurate for you.
- **Go local four ways.** Where an idea says "Charlottesville," make an Albemarle County version plus one each for Charlottesville, Crozet and Glenmore.

## Standard CTA (use on every ad unless noted)

- **Button:** Get Quote (or Call Now)
- **Last line of the post:** "Tap **Get Quote** for a fair price in minutes. No visit, no pressure."

This covers what to do and what happens next. A quote over the phone in minutes is an ease benefit on its own, since no stranger has to come to the house just to give a price. "No pressure" is on purpose: Hawkins found pressure creates resistance, while an "okay either way" stance builds trust.

## The psychology layer (applied to all 15)

### 1. Meet them where they are, then lift them one level

From `Hawkins_Human_Psychology_Emotions_Decisions`: Courage (200) is the line where people act ("I can do it"). Below it, fear, guilt and pressure get attention, but they also cause resistance, regret and the feeling of being worked. The file's own ad section says to start where the buyer is and lift them one rung, ideally to Courage ("it's handled"), Acceptance ("I'm okay, my home's okay") or Love ("I'm taking care of what I love").

| What your buyer says | Their level | Lift them to | How the ads do it |
|---|---|---|---|
| "My windows are filthy. Filthy. Filthy." | Guilt / shame | Acceptance | "Happens to the nicest homes." Never shame them. |
| "I'm too old." "It's overwhelming." "You just get used to it." | Apathy | Courage | "You don't have to." One easy step. |
| "I can't reach the outsides." "You lose strength at my age." | Grief (lost ability) | Love | Keep loving the house. We'll take the high parts. |
| Ladders, strangers, damage, being let down again | Fear | Courage / trust | Name the fear, then relieve it in the same ad: safe method, real faces, the walk-through. |
| "Before the holiday." "It just needs to be done." | Desire | Courage | Make deciding easy: a price in minutes, no visit. |
| "They didn't do a good job at all." | Anger | Acceptance | "Fair. Here's how we're different." Validate once, don't dwell. |
| "Look at our nice house." | Pride | Joy / gratitude | Sell enjoying the home, never "better than the neighbors." |
| "I love my house. I'm not moving." | Courage and up | Affirm it | "Staying put is a great plan. We'll make it easy." |

**The feeling they're really buying.** Ask Hawkins' "What for?" four times: clean windows → the light comes back and the house looks cared for → "I'm still on top of things" → "I'm safe and independent in the home I love." Sell that last feeling.

### 2. Sell who they are, not just clean glass

Schwartz (*Breakthrough Advertising*, chapter 8): people buy roles, not just results, and your product can both help them play the role and acknowledge they already do. Hawkins: "being" messages ("you're the kind of person who…") beat "having" messages. Make hiring you a sign of who they already are.

| The role they want | A line that affirms it |
|---|---|
| The particular one, who notices details | "Good eye." · "Particular about your home? Good. So are we." |
| The steward, who takes care of what's theirs | "You've taken care of this house for years. Let us take the high parts." |
| Independent, still running their home their way | "Staying independent isn't doing everything yourself. It's choosing what you do." |
| The good neighbor, who supports local | "When you hire us, you're giving a neighbor your business." |
| The careful one, who checks before trusting | "Check our reviews first. We'd want you to." |
| The gracious host | "Your home. Your rules." |
| The connector whose neighbors ask for recommendations | "Your neighbors will ask who did your windows." |

We also flip their own words up a level: "picky" becomes "high standards," "too old for ladders" becomes "choosing what you do," and "I can't reach them" becomes "you don't have to."

### 3. Sell trust like a neighbor, not a salesman

- **Calm confidence, no hype.** Hawkins: pressure "is felt as emotional blackmail," and "others always know when we are out to exploit them." No fake countdowns, no "you'd be crazy not to."
- **Be okay either way.** "Not the right time? No problem, we'll be back" makes people trust you more, not less (Hawkins' boy/girl theory and shirt experiment).
- **Admit something small.** "It's slower than pressure washing. On purpose." A damaging admission makes the rest believable (Hormozi's ad checklist).
- **Show the faces, names, manners and the walk-through.** Specific things they can check beat adjectives every time.

### 4. Put social proof on every ad, not just the review ad

- **A trust strip on all 15** (the bottom text): `★★★★★ 47 five-star Google reviews · TideWater Window Cleaning`. Hormozi's Branding book ranks what other people say above what you say. The `Proof Checklist` agrees.
- **Hawkins' "company we keep":** people rise when they see others like them who had the same problem and got past it. Use real customers' words.
- **Use proximity:** neighbors, streets and neighborhood names.

### 5. What we took out

- Any line that could make someone feel ashamed of their house.
- Fear with no relief. Every fear hook now lifts to courage in the same ad.
- "Better than the neighbors" pride.
- Pushy urgency. The route ad's limit stays because it's real, but now it comes with "we'll be back."

**Quick check for any new ad:**
- Does it lift them one level?
- Does it affirm who they are?
- Does someone other than you vouch for it?
- Would it still work if you were truly "okay either way"?

### 1. The Question  ·  Apathy → Courage  ·  proven winner

- **Lift:** "I've been meaning to… I can't remember" (apathy) → "That was easy, it's handled" (courage). The new subline takes away the guilt of not remembering.
- **Identity:** The homeowner who takes care of what's theirs.
- **Trust and proof:** your real face (the "boy next door"), "you're in good company" (other people are in the same boat), the trust strip.
- **Image:** You in your work shirt holding a squeegee in front of a real local home, looking at the camera. Warm, open and relaxed, like a neighbor saying hi, not a salesman. A half-cleaned window behind you if you can get one.
- **Text on image:** "When's the last time you had your windows washed?" Unchanged: it's a proven winner, so don't touch it. New subline: "Can't remember? You're in good company." Plus the trust strip.
- **Post:**
  > Real quick question: when's the last time your windows were washed?
  > If you had to think about it, you're in good company. Most homeowners we talk to can't remember, and plenty have never had the outsides done at all.
  > You've taken good care of your home. This part's easy: a local crew that takes their shoes off, puts every screen back, and walks the house with you before we leave.
  > Tap Get Quote for a fair price in minutes. No visit, no pressure.
- **What changed:** same proven headline, plus a no-guilt subline with social proof, a line acknowledging how well they've cared for the home, and the trust strip.
- **Variations:** area versions in the subline and post ("Real quick question, Crozet:" / "…Glenmore:" / "…Charlottesville:") · "Can't remember? Neither could [first name] in [Crozet]." (a real customer, with their OK) · a crew photo instead of you · a different house.

- **Lift:** pride ("look at our nice house") → joy and gratitude in owning it. That's Hawkins' higher alternative to pride: "we enjoy its beauty… and we feel grateful for having it."
- **Identity:** Someone who chose well and loves their home. We acknowledge the choice.
- **Trust and proof:** a customer quote, the trust strip.
- **Image:** Same view through spotless glass: the Blue Ridge from a Crozet home, or the rolling hills and mountains around Glenmore.
- **Text on image:** "You bought the house for this view." Subline: "Let's get it back." Plus the trust strip.
- **Post:**
  > You picked this house for a reason. Maybe the view, maybe the morning light in the kitchen.
  > "Every window and screen looks fantastic and we can see the beautiful outdoors again!" That's what one customer told us after her visit, and it's the goal every time.
  > Tap Get Quote for a fair price in minutes. No visit, no pressure.
- **What changed:** The copy is about enjoying what they already chose, not showing it off.

## Production cheat sheet for statics

- **Real photos of real local jobs, shot on your phone,** beat stock photos and graphics. Ogilvy found photos beat drawings, the `Proof Checklist` says raw beats polished, and your buyers want to see homes that look like theirs.
- **One focus per image.** One window, one person, one ladder. No crowd scenes.
- **Few words on the image:** about 10 or fewer, big and high-contrast. Frame what they're looking at with as few words as possible.
- **Headline placement:** test the top third of the image (Hormozi) against right under the photo (Ogilvy).
- **People who look like your buyers:** homeowners 45 to 85, mostly women, plus your young, friendly crew.
- **Small logo and "Charlottesville" on every ad,** so the 80% who never read the post still know who and where you are.
- **Imply motion:** mid-squeegee stroke, water drops, crew walking. Movement is on Hormozi's must-have list, even in a still.
- **Make every winner in three sizes:** 1:1 and 4:5 for the feed, 9:16 for Stories.

## How to test them

1. **Launch all 15** as separate ads to the same audience (homeowners in your service radius, 45+), with the same CTA and the same budget per ad.
2. **Judge on cost per booked job, not clicks.** Track what happens after the click (`Headline Engine`, rule 5). Hormozi's long-term-results lesson: an ad with pricier leads that book bigger jobs and refer neighbors wins.
3. **After 1 to 2 weeks, keep the top 2 or 3** and turn off the rest.
4. **Run the winners through the Kaleidoscope** (`ACQ Advertising Handbook`), 5 to 10 versions each:
   - *Remix (no new photo):* new headline, new background color, a thin border, new font, new crop/size, black-and-white or higher-contrast filter.
   - *Remake (new photo):* same idea at a new house, new neighborhood name, new person, new prop, a different season.
5. **Going forward, use 70-20-10:** 70% of new ads are versions of winners, 20% borrow winning ideas from other industries, and 10% are wild new tests from Section 9 above.
6. **Don't retire a winner because you're bored of it.** Your prospects haven't seen it yet (Henry Ford's Model T story).
